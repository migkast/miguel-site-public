A practical guide to scope, deliverables, and realistic outcomes when you hire an ecommerce growth advisor for a three-month engagement.

Updated on: 2026-10-06

The most common thing I hear in a first call is some version of "we're spending more and getting less, and nobody can tell me why." The dashboards look fine. The agency sends reports. The team is busy. And growth has gone flat anyway. When that happens, the instinct is usually to add activity: a new channel, a new agency, a new tool. The first 90 days of a good advisory engagement should do the opposite. It should reduce the noise, confirm what the numbers actually support, and point at the one thing holding the business back.

Here is what a good three-month advisory should accomplish, what it should produce, and where it stops being able to promise anything.

What the first 90 days should actually establish

A good advisory engagement uses the first 90 days to answer four questions: what does profitable growth mean for this business, which numbers can you trust, what is the real constraint, and can the existing team act on a short plan with clear owners. The output is better decisions and cleaner evidence, not a guaranteed revenue lift.

In the version of this I run, the client keeps its growth leader, team, and agencies. I work above execution: setting commercial targets, diagnosing the constraint, prioritizing channels and budget, assigning ownership, and building a review rhythm the team can keep running after I leave. That framing matters because advisory and outsourced execution are different things, and confusing them wastes everyone's time.

Phase What good progress looks like How you know it worked
Weeks 1-4: baseline Agree on the commercial question the work must answer. Understand the model, targets, team, agencies, channels, and reporting. Reconcile the commerce platform, finance, analytics, and ad-platform views enough to know which numbers hold up. A stated business question (for example, can acquisition scale without eroding contribution margin), a baseline with real definitions, and a list of material data gaps.
Weeks 4-8: diagnosis Decide whether the binding constraint is economics, measurement, acquisition, conversion, retention, or execution. Pick a small number of moves that fit the evidence and the team's capacity. Name owners and decision rules. A diagnosis that separates root cause from symptom, and a short plan (fewer than ten high-confidence moves) with expected effect, effort, owner, and a way to judge results.
Weeks 8-12: act and decide Work with the existing specialists to put the top priority into practice. Review regularly. Adjust budget or direction when evidence warrants. Close with a clear view of what was learned and what is still uncertain. Visible ownership, a working review cadence, and conclusions that separate observed movement from proven causal impact.

These are sensible phases, not a published week-by-week contract. The engagement runs three months with weekly or biweekly working sessions, and the cadence adapts to the situation. I don't promise fixed day-30 or day-60 milestones, because the data quality and implementation speed vary too much between brands to pretend otherwise.

The evidence a good advisor helps you build

Most of the first month is unglamorous reconciliation work. You cannot prioritize anything until you know which numbers are real.

Commercial guardrails first. Before anyone optimizes a campaign, the business needs to define what profitable growth permits: contribution margin, acquisition boundaries, payback expectations, cash requirements, and customer value. Channel-reported ROAS is not the goal. It is one input, and often a misleading one. If you have never written down your profitable ecommerce targets, that is usually the first gap worth closing.

A metric set that changes decisions. Depending on the business, the useful numbers are contribution margin, new-customer CAC or cost per order, payback, LTV, MER, channel ROAS, and cash position. The aim is a small set that actually moves a decision, not a bigger dashboard nobody reads.

Measurement confidence. This is where a lot of engagements earn their keep. Compare commerce-platform orders against finance data, GA4, and ad-platform reporting. Document the discrepancies rather than quietly picking the friendliest figure. Common issues I keep finding: duplicate purchase events, missing transaction IDs, refunds not handled, and inconsistent treatment of tax, shipping, and discounts. Google's own guidance on measuring ecommerce in GA4 describes using a transaction_id to avoid counting purchases twice and a refund event to measure returns. Validating events in DebugView tells you whether the feed works. It does not make the analytics number the definitive profit figure.

Channel role and customer quality. Judge each channel against the job it is supposed to do and its contribution to new customers and profit, not just the revenue each platform claims. Platform-reported conversions are not the same as incremental sales. ROAS can improve because you are capturing demand that already existed, which looks like growth on a chart and isn't.

The whole system. Acquisition, conversion, retention, and team execution interact. A bad result does not automatically indict the channel or the agency. It might be unclear economics, thin creative supply, checkout friction, broken tracking, or nobody actually owning the number. Deciding which requires looking at the system, not one report.

Where advisory ends and leadership begins

This is the distinction people get wrong most often, so it is worth being blunt.

Advisory keeps your growth leader, team, and agencies in place. My job is to help them make better decisions and give direction. I am not a second pair of hands running every ad account day to day. If what a business really needs is someone to own goals, budgets, and accountability directly, that is a fractional head of growth arrangement, which is a different engagement with a different level of responsibility.

The honest version of this: if you have a capable team that lacks direction and a decision rhythm, advisory fits. If you have no one who can own the growth function and execute against a plan, advisory alone will not fix that. A great diagnosis handed to a team that cannot act on it is just a nicer version of the problem you started with.

What a 90-day engagement cannot promise

I would rather lose a deal than oversell one, so here are the limits.

An audit produces a hypothesis, not proof. A growth audit on its own cannot establish causation. Where test design, traffic volume, or timing support a clean experiment, run one. Where they don't, report the result as uncertain. Google frames incrementality testing as comparing a business metric for people exposed to ads against a group that wasn't. If you cannot structure that comparison, you cannot claim a proven lift, and you should not.

Attribution is a model, not a ledger. GA4 offers data-driven and last-click models, among others. Data-driven attribution distributes credit across the path; paid-and-organic last click hands it to the final non-direct touch. Different channel reports are built on different assumptions, so treating them as directly comparable profit measures produces decisions built on sand.

External benchmarks are prompts, not targets. Baymard's checkout research reports that a large share of shoppers abandon because they are only browsing or not ready to buy, while others cite extra costs and checkout problems. Useful context. It cannot tell you that checkout is your biggest constraint. Only your own data can do that.

No fixed revenue guarantee. Scope, data quality, implementation capacity, and the time needed to see effects all vary. What a good engagement reliably produces is diagnosis, direction, coaching, and a decision rhythm. Anyone promising a specific percentage lift in 90 days is either lucky or guessing.

Who this fits, and who it doesn't

The advisory is built for established ecommerce brands roughly in the $2M to $20M range: companies with a team and real acquisition activity, where growth has become hard to direct. If you are pre-product-market-fit or spending a few thousand a month, this is the wrong tool. The format is deliberately selective because the work is hands-on and the calendar is limited.

If growth has flattened and you are not sure whether the problem sits with the agency, the team, or the strategy, the first step is usually to diagnose stalled ecommerce growth before committing budget to any fix. More spend on an undiagnosed constraint just makes the constraint more expensive.

How to judge whether the engagement worked

The success test is not a deck. It is not a longer backlog. And it is definitely not a short-term bump in platform ROAS, which can move for reasons that have nothing to do with the health of the business.

A good 90 days leaves you with four things:

  • Commercially grounded targets you can defend to your finance team.
  • A credible account of what your data can and cannot tell you.
  • A prioritized plan with named owners and clear decision rules.
  • A repeatable review-and-decision process the team keeps running after the engagement ends.

If you have those, you can make better calls for the next year regardless of who is advising you. That is the actual deliverable. The recommendations matter, but the decision quality that outlasts the engagement matters more.

FAQ

How long before I see results from a growth advisory?

Depends entirely on the constraint. If the binding issue is measurement or economics, you get clearer decisions within weeks but the revenue effect shows up later, once the team acts on a plan. If the constraint is something testable in-platform, you might see directional movement inside the three months. Be skeptical of anyone who quotes a timeline before diagnosing your constraint.

Will an advisor replace my agency or in-house team?

In an advisory engagement, no. The model keeps your existing team and agencies and works above them, setting direction and holding the plan accountable. Replacing people only makes sense when a specific capability is missing or a relationship is genuinely broken, and that is a separate decision from hiring an advisor.

What is the difference between a growth advisory and a fractional head of growth?

Advisory gives you diagnosis, direction, and coaching while your team executes. A fractional head of growth takes direct responsibility for goals, budgets, and accountability. If you have a capable team that needs direction, advisory fits. If you need someone to own the function, the fractional arrangement is the better match.

Can 90 days really fix stalled growth?

It can identify why growth stalled and set up the team to fix it, which is often the harder part. Whether the fix fully lands inside 90 days depends on the constraint and your implementation speed. Treat the engagement as establishing better decisions and evidence, not as a guaranteed revenue result.
