How to think about advisory and fractional leadership fees when the numbers you get quoted range from $6,000 to $50,000 a month.

Updated on: 2026-09-28

The first question most founders ask me is some version of "what does this cost?" The honest answer is that the fee is the easy part. The hard part is figuring out what you are actually buying, whether you need it, and how you will know it worked. A $6,000 monthly retainer can be expensive if it buys you a second opinion you ignore. A $20,000 seat can be cheap if it stops you from burning $40,000 a month on channels that never paid back.

If you want a single number, here it is with the caveat attached: published ecommerce growth advisor and fractional leadership fees currently run from roughly $6,000 to $50,000 a month, with three-month to twelve-month commitments common. But those figures cover completely different jobs. Some are twice-monthly advice calls. Some are a person directing your agencies, budgets, and internal specialists every week. Comparing them as if they were the same unit of service is where most buyers go wrong.

What "growth advisor" actually means when you pay for it

The word gets stretched to cover mentors, paid-media agencies, implementation vendors, and senior operators who help you decide what to do. Those are not interchangeable, and the price only makes sense once you know which one you are hiring.

Three buying situations show up over and over:

  • You have competent people running channels, but nobody is setting priorities, checking the numbers, or holding the plan together. You need decisions and leadership.
  • You know what to do but lack the hands to execute. You need capacity, which usually means an agency or a hire.
  • You need both, at least for a while.

Most brands I talk to think they have a capacity problem. When we dig in, they have a decision problem wearing a capacity costume. The team is busy. Everyone is optimizing their piece. Nobody owns whether the whole thing is profitable. Adding more execution to that does not fix it. It just makes the misalignment more expensive.

This is the distinction that changes what a fair price looks like. Buy the missing capability, not the label.

The gap between $6,000 and $50,000 is not a quality ladder. It is a difference in seat, authority, and time. A 2026 fractional CMO cost guide puts the outer range at $3,000 to $50,000 a month, which tells you mostly that the category is not standardized. Published tiers like Taylor Sicard's $6,000 to $25,000 structure are asking prices for defined scopes, not a going rate you can hold up against every quote.

Advisory versus fractional leadership: what you are really choosing

The cleanest way I know to frame the decision is by decision authority and time commitment.

Advisory improves the quality of your decisions without adding a body to your org chart. My three-month advisory is built around your existing growth leader, team, and agencies. I diagnose the real constraint, set commercial targets and payback expectations, sort out channel roles and budget priorities, evaluate whether your agencies and people are actually good, and install a review rhythm you can run yourselves. It is a poor fit if what you mainly need is more hands to execute. It is a strong fit if you have hands and no direction.

Fractional leadership buys ownership. As a Fractional Head of Growth, I direct the growth function: set goals and budgets, run the weekly reviews, point agencies and specialists at the right work, and carry accountability for the plan. That is a heavier seat and it costs more, because it is more of my week and more of my name attached to the result.

If you are weighing these two against a full-time hire or an agency, I have written a longer breakdown of the fractional versus agency versus in-house tradeoffs that goes past pricing into ownership and speed.

One thing I want to be direct about: I work with a maximum of five clients at a time. That constraint is the whole reason the work stays hands-on. It also means the price reflects genuine capacity, not a template being copied across forty accounts.

Why the fee is the wrong thing to optimize first

Buyers fixate on the monthly rate and skip the two numbers that actually determine ROI: the size of the decision at stake, and the cost of getting it wrong.

If you are spending $30,000 a month on paid media and your ROAS falls apart every time you scale, the recurring cost of that unsolved problem dwarfs any advisory fee. The advisor's job is to make that constraint visible and fixable. Against that, arguing over whether the retainer is $8,000 or $12,000 is optimizing the small number.

The mistake I see is treating the fee as the total investment. It is not. When you test whether an engagement paid back, you have to include everything the plan required: my fee, plus any incremental agency work, creative production, software, and internal labor the plan set in motion. An advisor who "only" costs $10,000 a month but triggers $30,000 in new execution spend has to be judged against the full $40,000.

How to actually calculate the ROI

Use incremental contribution profit as the hurdle, not attributed revenue. Attributed revenue flatters everyone. Contribution profit is what pays for the engagement.

The clean formula, following the way Shopify frames ROI versus ROAS, is:

(incremental profit − total investment) ÷ total investment × 100

ROI is built on net profit and total investment. ROAS is just ad revenue divided by ad spend, which is a channel metric, not a business metric. Do not let a healthy ROAS convince you the business made money.

Here is a worked example. Illustration, not a benchmark. A three-month engagement at $8,000 a month costs $24,000 before any other spend. At a 40% contribution margin, you would need $60,000 in incremental net revenue to generate $24,000 in contribution profit, which just covers the fee before any other incremental investment. The whole thing depends on two honest inputs: a genuinely incremental result, and your real contribution margin. Fudge either and the math becomes theater.

For acquisition work, track CAC and payback alongside contribution profit. Shopify defines CAC as sales and marketing spend divided by customers acquired, and CAC payback as CAC divided by average monthly gross profit per customer. Those two make gross profit and recovery time more decision-useful than a revenue growth chart, which can hide unprofitable acquisition for a long time.

And separate platform-reported ROAS from business-level ROI. Google Ads notes that conversion values can represent business value like revenue or profit margins, but the number only means something if the values and conversion setup behind it are correct. Ask any advisor how they plan to reconcile platform reporting with your actual financials, and whether the plan accounts for incrementality or just credited conversions. This is where real technical depth matters, because first-party attribution and server-side tracking are the difference between numbers you can bet on and numbers you hope are right.

What to nail down before you sign

Get scope and commercial terms in writing. The fee is meaningless without them.

  • Cadence and access. How often are decisions reviewed? Weekly operating leadership and a biweekly advice call are not the same product.
  • What the provider personally does between meetings. This is the question that separates a sounding board from an operator.
  • Who owns follow-through. If nobody owns it, nothing happens, and you paid for a nice document.
  • Minimum term and cancellation. A fixed term raises the stakes on defining success early. Compare the commitment against the time it takes to diagnose, implement, and observe results, not just the monthly rate.
  • What is billed separately. Large one-off scopes are often quoted outside the retainer.
  • Whether the fee covers the named person or a team. With me, it covers me. With some agencies and firms, it covers whoever they staff.

On diagnostics: some providers offer a paid, fixed-fee read before you commit, sometimes credited toward a later engagement. My discovery call works differently. It is a fit conversation, not a free audit and not a paid deliverable. If the fit is real, we talk about the right engagement and the price after I understand your business and the level of involvement you need.

What I would do first

Before you request a single quote, do three things.

Write down the specific decision or constraint you are stuck on. "Growth is stalling" is not it. "ROAS drops below 1.8 every time we push Meta past $20k a day and we don't know why" is. If you cannot name the constraint, that itself might be the thing to buy help diagnosing. I have a longer method for that in the guide to diagnosing stalled ecommerce growth.

Pull your real contribution margin and current CAC payback. You need these to test any ROI claim, and honestly, a lot of brands discover their margins are thinner than the dashboard suggested the moment they do this.

Decide whether you need better decisions or more hands. Be strict with yourself. The answer determines whether advisory, fractional leadership, an agency, or a hire is the right purchase, and it changes what a fair price even means.

When reviewing proposals, ask what the advisor expects to deliver in the first 90 days of a growth advisory so you can evaluate progress against the fee.

FAQ

How much does an ecommerce growth advisor cost?

Published fees currently run from about $6,000 to $50,000 a month, but that range covers very different jobs. My advisory and fractional leadership are priced per engagement after a fit conversation, because a three-month diagnosis with a review rhythm and a full fractional leadership seat are not the same amount of my week. Any quote only makes sense against the scope, cadence, and authority attached to it.

Is a growth advisor worth it for a brand under $5M?

Sometimes, but be careful. Below roughly $2M, the fee often eats too much of your margin and a strong operator hire may pay back faster. My work is generally best suited to brands from around $2M to $20M. Revenue is a screening clue, not proof of fit. Team structure, channel complexity, and the cost of a wrong decision matter more than the topline number.

What's the difference between paying for advisory versus fractional leadership?

Advisory improves your decisions while your existing team and agencies keep executing. Fractional leadership means someone actually owns the growth function: setting budgets, directing agencies, running the weekly reviews, and carrying the result. Advisory is the lighter, cheaper seat. Fractional is heavier and costs more because it is more time and more accountability.

How do I measure ROI on a growth advisor?

Use incremental contribution profit against total investment, not attributed revenue. Include the fee plus any execution, software, and labor the plan required. Set a baseline and agree how success is measured before work starts. If you skip the baseline, you will never separate what the advisor changed from what would have happened anyway.

Should I expect a free audit before committing?

Not necessarily, and I would be skeptical of one. A serious diagnosis takes real work, so it is either a paid step or it happens inside the engagement. My discovery call is a fit conversation to see whether we should work together, not a free strategy audit dressed up as a sales call.
