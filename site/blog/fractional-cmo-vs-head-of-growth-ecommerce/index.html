A practical guide to choosing the right fractional leader for a $2M-$20M ecommerce brand, based on what's actually missing.

Updated on: 2026-09-17

If your brand already has products, traction, agencies, and a marketing manager or two, and growth still feels stuck, the fastest way to pick the wrong hire is to shop by title. A fractional CMO and a fractional head of growth can look similar on paper. They can even do overlapping work. But they answer different questions, and hiring the one who answers the wrong question is an expensive way to stay stuck for another two quarters.

Here is the short version: choose a fractional CMO when the missing thing is senior ownership of the whole marketing function, meaning positioning, brand, demand, budget, team design, and executive alignment. Choose a fractional head of growth when the missing thing is an accountable operator for the growth system, meaning acquisition, conversion, retention, economics, experimentation, and the weekly decisions that keep agencies and specialists pointed at the same commercial target.

If you can't name which one is missing yet, that's a real answer too, and it usually points to a short diagnostic before you commit to either.

The titles are not standardized, so stop treating them like they are

Neither "fractional CMO" nor "fractional head of growth" is a fixed professional category. One company's fractional CMO is a strategic advisor who joins a monthly meeting. Another's is an embedded part-time executive building a department. Some "heads of growth" are marketing-led. Others sit between product, data, and engineering with experimentation as the center of the job.

What this means in practice: you cannot hire from the title. You hire from a written charter that says who owns which decisions. I've watched brands sign a "fractional CMO" expecting hands-on paid media leadership, then discover the person doesn't touch campaigns and doesn't want to. The disappointment wasn't about the person. It was about a contract nobody wrote down.

So before any of the comparison below matters, write the charter. Then compare charters, not job titles.

What each role actually owns

Here's the directional split. It's not absolute. A CMO can own experimentation, a head of growth can influence positioning, and the good ones flex. But the center of gravity is different.

Decision criterion Fractional CMO Fractional Head of Growth
Primary mandate Lead the whole marketing function and connect it to company strategy Improve the measurable growth system and keep execution tied to commercial outcomes
Typical scope Brand, positioning, messaging, demand, customer experience, marketing ops, budget, team, agencies, executive comms Acquisition, conversion, retention, lifecycle, experimentation, analytics, channel economics, agencies, specialists
Problem it solves "Marketing lacks senior leadership or strategic coherence" "Everyone is busy, but growth has no accountable owner"
Brand ownership Central Secondary unless explicitly included
Paid acquisition Sets strategy, budget, targets, oversight Directly involved in priorities, testing, scaling rules, reviews
CRO May oversee it inside the plan Usually a core lever: landing pages, product pages, checkout, offers
Retention/lifecycle Oversees email, SMS, loyalty, CRM Treats retention and customer value as core growth metrics
Experimentation May sponsor tests; not always central Normally owns the backlog, cadence, and prioritization
Success measures Marketing-sourced revenue, brand health, CAC, retention, marketing ROI Profitable revenue, new-customer CAC, contribution margin, conversion, LTV, payback, experiment impact
Strongest fit Brand needs a senior marketing department leader Brand has marketing resources but no accountable growth direction
Main risk Role gets too broad to fix execution quickly Role over-indexes on short-term performance, neglects brand and demand

The pattern I keep seeing at the $2M-$20M range: the brand doesn't lack a marketing department. It lacks someone who connects paid media, CRO, retention, data, and three agencies into one plan with one number attached to it. That's a growth problem more often than a CMO problem.

When the CMO is the right call

A fractional CMO earns the seat when the problem is bigger than paid media or funnel efficiency. Real signals:

  • You're repositioning, moving upmarket, or entering a new market.
  • You're launching a new category or product line and the story needs to be built, not just amplified.
  • Differentiation is weak and you're leaning on discounts to move product.
  • There is no senior marketing owner at all, and the founder is effectively acting as the marketing executive.
  • You need someone to design the marketing org, decide who to hire, and set the long-term structure.

The CMO brief should include positioning and market strategy, the full marketing portfolio across paid, organic, lifecycle, creative, and partnerships, budget allocation across channels, brand stewardship so performance activity doesn't quietly erode pricing power, and executive alignment that translates marketing into revenue, margin, and cash for the CEO and finance.

Where a CMO is a poor fit: your positioning is already clear, your immediate pain is executional accountability, and you need someone to make weekly calls on budgets, CRO, and retention rather than run a brand strategy sprint. Hiring a strategist to solve an operating problem produces a lovely deck and the same flat revenue.

When the head of growth is the right call

The head of growth fits when you have activity but no accountable owner for the system. The charter is narrower and more operational:

  1. Commercial target and growth model. Define the growth number and the boundaries of profitable acquisition. Connect revenue, contribution margin, CAC, payback, repeat purchase, and cash into one view instead of ten dashboards that disagree.
  2. Diagnosis. Figure out whether the real constraint is channel performance, creative supply, conversion rate, offer strategy, retention, attribution and data quality, agency capability, internal ownership, or inventory and cash. Most brands guess at this and spend against the wrong one.
  3. Channel and budget priorities. Give each channel a job. Set scale, hold, and cut rules. Stop each specialist from optimizing their isolated metric while the blended number sinks.
  4. Experimentation. Prioritize tests across creative, audiences, pages, offers, checkout, and lifecycle, then run a real review process. High test volume is not success. Useful learning and commercial impact are.
  5. Execution leadership. Direct agencies and internal specialists, set owners, deadlines, decision rights, and escalation rules, run weekly growth reviews, and train the existing team instead of automatically replacing it.
  6. Measurement. Decide which numbers you can trust. Reconcile platform reporting against ecommerce, finance, and customer data. Use blended and cohort measures where platform attribution falls short.

This is the closer fit for most brands in the target range, because the honest bottleneck is rarely "we have no marketing." It's "nobody owns whether the marketing works."

The ROAS trap that decides more of these hires than people admit

Be careful with anyone, CMO or head of growth, who defines success mainly through platform-reported ROAS. Platform ROAS quietly hides returning-customer revenue counted as new, discounts, shipping and fulfillment costs, returns, payment fees, and agency and creative costs. It also blends new and existing customers into one flattering number.

For ecommerce, contribution margin, blended CAC, cohort LTV, payback, and repeat purchase belong next to channel metrics, not behind them. Contribution margin is generally revenue after product and variable fulfillment costs. Blended CAC is total acquisition spend divided by new customers. The exact cost layers and time windows are worth arguing about before the engagement starts, because that argument is where "healthy dashboard, weak margin" gets caught. This economic framing is the part that most cleanly separates a growth operator from a media manager.

Ecommerce-specific tiebreakers

Brand complexity. Repositioning, new markets, discount dependence, weak differentiation lean CMO. Falling paid efficiency, weak site conversion, disconnected agency work, low repeat purchase lean head of growth.

Existing team. No senior marketing owner and a need to build the function lean CMO. Existing agencies, media buyers, lifecycle specialists, and developers who lack a shared plan lean head of growth.

Technical depth. If you need a structured experiment backlog, CRO ownership, better attribution, reliable first-party data, and someone who can tell a media problem apart from a measurement problem, that's growth territory, sitting between marketing, product, data, and engineering.

Founder need. A CMO answers "who are we for and why should the market choose us." A head of growth answers "what's actually blocking growth, which channel gets the next dollar, is the agency performing, and who owns the next action."

If you want someone who joins a monthly call and reviews a deck, this isn't that. If you want someone accountable for the growth number and in the weekly reviews, it is.

Among the paths available to a $2M-$20M brand with stalled growth, capable agencies, and no accountable owner, this is the cleaner match than a conventional brand-led fractional CMO, precisely because the constraint at that stage is usually direction and economics, not brand strategy. If your real problem is positioning or building a marketing department from zero, a traditional fractional CMO is the more honest recommendation, and you should hire for that instead.

What I'd do before signing anything

Don't start with "CMO or head of growth." Start with the constraint. If you can't yet say whether growth is blocked by channel, conversion, retention, measurement, agency capability, or strategy, buy the diagnosis first. A three-month advisory that preserves your existing team and hands you a prioritized plan is a cheaper way to find out than a twelve-month leadership retainer aimed at the wrong problem.

Then write the charter. Then ask the questions below. The answers tell you more than any title ever will.

FAQ

Is a fractional head of growth just a cheaper fractional CMO?

No, and assuming that is how brands overpay for the wrong scope. They're different jobs, not the same job at two price points. A head of growth focused on execution accountability can cost more than a light-touch advisory CMO, or less than an embedded CMO building a department. Price tracks seniority, time, team responsibility, technical involvement, and whether the person is accountable for execution or only advising. Compare charters and accountability, not assumed rate cards.

What questions actually reveal which role I need?

Ask what decisions the person owns without founder approval, whether they own brand and positioning or only growth performance, whether they direct agencies and specialists, whether they execute or lead the people executing, which single metric is the primary accountability number, how new and returning customers are separated, who owns CRO and experimentation, and what happens when the problem turns out to be creative, inventory, pricing, or data rather than media. The gaps in the answers show you the role.

Can one person do both?

Sometimes, for a while, at a smaller scale. A strong operator can cover growth execution and lend real judgment on positioning. The risk is time. A fractional role is a fraction of a week, and a charter that spans brand strategy, org design, weekly performance decisions, and experimentation is usually too broad to move any of them fast. If you genuinely need both, sequence them or staff them, rather than hoping one part-time person absorbs a full-time mandate.

We already have a head of marketing. Do we still need a fractional leader?

Maybe not a leader. If you have a capable growth lead who's missing an outside diagnosis, clearer commercial targets, or coaching, an advisory engagement often beats adding another layer of leadership. Adding a fractional executive on top of a competent internal owner can blur accountability instead of fixing it. Hire leadership when there's a leadership gap, not when there's a decision-quality gap.
