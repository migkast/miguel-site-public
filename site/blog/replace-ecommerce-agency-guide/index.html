When to Replace Your Ecommerce Agency (and When Not To)

Learn when to replace your ecommerce agency and when to fix briefs, targets, or measurement instead. Avoid common mistakes and make informed decisions.

A practitioner's guide to separating an agency failure from a broken brief, unrealistic targets, or measurement you can't trust.

Updated on: 2026-09-18

Replace the agency when there is a repeated, evidenced failure on something it was actually hired and equipped to control, and it cannot or will not fix it after you have made the brief, targets, access, and measurement clear. Fix the brief, targets, or measurement first when the agency is being judged against unclear ownership, unrealistic economics, unreliable tracking, or conversion and retention problems outside its scope. A bad result alone is not enough. The first question is always whether the agency failed at something inside its control.

Most founders skip that question. They see ROAS dropping while spend climbs, they feel the frustration build over a few reporting cycles, and they start interviewing replacements. Sometimes that's right. More often the new agency inherits the same broken brief and the same untrustworthy numbers, and eight months later the founder is back in the same room having the same conversation with a different logo on the deck.

The mistake I keep seeing

The pattern is almost always the same. A brand hires an agency to "scale Meta" or "improve ROAS." No period. No margin floor. No agreed CAC. No split between new and returning customers. The agency optimizes toward platform ROAS because that's the only number anyone agreed on, and platform ROAS looks fine while the business quietly loses money on every new order.

Then growth stalls, the founder blames the agency, and the replacement search begins. The problem was never the agency. It was that nobody defined what profitable growth meant, so the agency optimized the only target it was given.

This is why the work starts with diagnosis, not termination. Before you fire anyone, you need to know whether you're looking at a business problem wearing an agency costume. Revenue and conversion rate deteriorate for reasons that have nothing to do with media buying: pricing, stock-outs, delivery times, a weak offer, checkout friction, broken tracking, softening demand, thinning margins. A capable agency can't fix any of those, and a new one won't either.

The core diagnostic: business performance vs agency performance

Run these questions before you decide anything. Each one moves you toward "keep diagnosing the agency" or "fix your own foundations first."

Question If yes Likely direction
Was the commercial objective clear (outcome, timeframe, market, budget, constraints)? Agency had a real target Keep diagnosing agency execution
Were targets economically realistic (CAC, payback, contribution margin, new-customer value)? Target problem less likely Continue
Do the numbers reconcile with the backend (revenue, refunds, new vs returning)? Measurement less likely to blame Continue
Did the agency control the failing lever (its campaigns, creative testing, feed, reporting)? Accountability is stronger Continue
Did it have the inputs (budget, creative volume, inventory, approvals, access)? Failure more attributable to agency Continue
Did it diagnose, test, document, and change course? Progress visible Keep if improving, replace if not
Did the same failure persist after a written reset? Foundations fixed, delivery still poor Replacement justified

If you answer "no" to the early questions, your problem is upstream of the agency. Stop the replacement search and fix the thing you actually control.

When to fix the brief first

A brief needs fixing when it describes activity instead of a decision. "Scale Meta" is activity. "Grow profitable new customers at or below €40 CAC with 3-month payback in the UK and DE markets through Q1" is a decision the agency can be held to.

Signs your brief is the problem:

  • It names a metric the agency can't control (blended revenue, site-wide conversion rate) as the primary KPI.
  • Different teams are chasing conflicting goals. The agency is rewarded for platform ROAS while the business needs profitable acquisition.
  • It assumes more spend can solve a product, pricing, inventory, or retention problem.
  • Priorities change weekly with no acknowledgment of what that does to learning and delivery.
  • There's no baseline, no decision rules, no test plan, no review cadence.

A good brief defines the business decision the agency is expected to improve, not the work it should perform. Google's own guidance frames a useful objective as clear, measurable, and time-bound, which is a low bar most briefs still fail.

Rewrite the brief around the commercial objective, the economic boundaries (allowable CAC, payback period, contribution-margin floor, promo assumptions), the outcomes the agency actually controls, leading indicators like creative test volume and checkout progression, clear decision rights, and a review rhythm with a documented action log. Not a monthly report that narrates what already happened.

When the targets are the real problem

Challenge the target before you blame the agency when:

  • Required ROAS is calculated from revenue and ignores contribution margin.
  • The target assumes first-order profitability when your model depends on repeat purchase.
  • It uses blended CAC while the agency runs one channel.
  • It counts returning-customer revenue as proof of acquisition efficiency.
  • It ignores discounting, shipping, refunds, taxes, payment fees, or returns.
  • It's borrowed from an industry benchmark instead of your own historical baseline.
  • It demands a spend level your audience, creative supply, inventory, or conversion rate can't support.

A channel can post attractive ROAS while the business bleeds. An agency can look like it missed a short-term ROAS target while generating genuinely valuable customers whose payback lands later. The target has to state what kind of growth counts, for whom, and by when.

Keep a clear hierarchy and don't let the bottom substitute for the top:

Level Example Purpose
Business outcome Contribution profit, profitable revenue The management outcome
Customer economics New-customer CAC, payback, first-order margin Is acquisition viable
Channel outcome Incremental sales, qualified traffic Channel contribution
Execution indicator Creative tests shipped, feed errors fixed Is the work happening
Diagnostic metric CTR, add-to-cart, checkout completion Why the number moved

CTR and add-to-cart rate are diagnostic evidence. They are not proof of profitable growth, and no agency should be allowed to present them as if they were.

When measurement is the problem, not the agency

Fix measurement first if people are arguing over dashboards or if platform numbers don't reconcile with orders and finance. You cannot fairly judge an agency optimizing against data you don't trust.

Minimum checks for any ecommerce account:

  • The purchase event fires once per order, with a unique transaction ID.
  • Revenue, currency, tax, shipping, discounts, and refunds pass correctly.
  • Item IDs, quantities, and values match the store.
  • New and returning customers can be separated.
  • Channel naming and campaign parameters are consistent.
  • Refunds and cancellations aren't counted as retained revenue.
  • Platform-reported conversions aren't being compared to backend orders as if they were the same measure.

Google's GA4 ecommerce implementation guide covers the events that matter (view_item, add_to_cart, begin_checkout, add_payment_info, purchase, refund) and its funnel exploration guidance shows where users actually drop out. Building reliable first-party attribution and server-side tracking is where I spend a lot of early diagnostic time, because most disputes about "agency performance" dissolve once the numbers reconcile.

Attribution tells you which touchpoints got credit. It doesn't tell you what caused the sale. Don't replace an agency just because Meta and Google report different revenue, last-click undervalues prospecting, or attribution moved after a consent change. Agree which source answers which decision, then use backend revenue, contribution economics, platform diagnostics, and incrementality tests together.

When conversion or the customer experience is the constraint

An agency can send you good traffic while the site, offer, or checkout kills the sale. Watch for strong CTR with weak product-page engagement, healthy add-to-cart with brutal checkout drop-off, mobile conversion far below desktop with no audience explanation, traffic to out-of-stock or overpriced products, and surprise shipping or fees late in checkout.

The evidence here is hard to argue with. Baymard's checkout research puts the average cart abandonment rate around 70%, with checkout design and flow repeatedly identified as the sole cause of abandonment in their usability testing. Inspect the funnel by device, product, geography, and customer type before you touch the agency contract. If the agency owns CRO and landing pages, it may still be accountable. If the issue is pricing, fulfillment, or payment infrastructure, it's not the agency's problem to solve.

When replacement is warranted

Replacement gets stronger when you see a pattern of agency-side failure with the foundations already sound:

  1. It can't explain performance. Reports platform metrics but can't connect them to business revenue, margin, new-customer economics, or a prioritized action plan.
  2. It hides behind attribution. Changes windows or models to protect a story, claims credit finance can't validate, refuses to separate platform-reported from incremental results.
  3. It doesn't do the agreed work. Missed deliverables, late reporting, unexecuted tests, neglected tracking or feed issues, scope quietly shrinking without a change request.
  4. It doesn't learn. Weak results are survivable. What matters is whether the agency forms hypotheses, runs real tests, records what changed, and stops repeating what fails. Persistent failure without learning is the serious signal.
  5. It lacks the capability you bought. Missing platform expertise, no creative testing capacity, no data engineering, no senior involvement. Test this against the agreed scope, not against things it was never hired for.
  6. The relationship is unsafe. Dishonesty, data manipulation, poor access governance, refusal to hand over accounts or assets, or key-person changes that remove the capability you were paying for. That's a trust failure, not a performance miss, and it justifies moving faster.

A clear scope-of-work document matters here, because without one you may read a missed outcome as a delivery failure when the agency understood the engagement differently.

The reset before replace test

Unless there's misconduct, a security issue, or a contractual breach, run a short written remediation period before terminating.

Give the agency a document with the agreed commercial objective, the baseline period and data sources, primary and secondary KPIs, economic boundaries, scope and exclusions, named owners, required resources, reporting cadence, the specific failures to correct, deadlines, and the review date with consequences.

Ask the agency to respond with its diagnosis of the constraint, what it accepts or disputes, the tests it will run, what it needs from you, and the evidence and timing it expects. This is where you find out fast whether you have a capable partner being badly managed or a genuine execution problem.

Judge the reset on measurement quality, delivery against scope, speed and quality of diagnosis, completed tests, improved funnel indicators, and commercial results over an appropriate window. Don't demand an overnight revenue turnaround if the agency doesn't control the whole funnel. If it meets the reset but results stay poor because of price, demand, stock, or checkout, the agency isn't your problem. If it misses the reset or keeps obscuring the evidence, replacement is justified.

A practical decision matrix

Evidence after diagnosis Action
Tracking unreliable, agency optimizing against it Fix measurement, suspend performance conclusions
Revenue-only target while margin and payback erode Rebuild targets around commercial economics
Strong traffic, severe checkout drop-off Fix the funnel; replace only if agency owns it and failed to act
No clear scope, ownership, or decision rights Reset the brief and responsibilities
Clear brief, reliable data, resources, repeated execution failures Start formal replacement or a final improvement plan
Attractive platform ROAS, no proof of profitable growth Require finance-reconciled measurement; replace if refused
Credible diagnosis, acts, learns even before revenue moves Continue with explicit milestones
Dishonest, obstructive, refuses access Protect accounts, follow the contract, plan an orderly exit

What I would do first

Before any decision, pull one review pack: the contract and SOW, the original brief and approved changes, agreed targets and their assumptions, backend orders and revenue, a contribution-margin view, platform spend and conversion data, a GA4 event-quality audit, the checkout funnel by device and customer type, and the creative testing log. Add meeting notes, unresolved actions, and staffing changes.

The point isn't to build a case against the agency. It's to separate bad outcomes the agency caused from bad outcomes that expose a wider growth constraint. Most of the time, assembling this pack answers the question on its own.

A few caveats worth holding onto. There's no universal timeframe that proves an agency should go. The right evaluation window depends on traffic, purchase cycle, seasonality, spend, and how long changes take to affect repeat purchase. A short-term dip is fine during a deliberate tracking migration, a creative reset, or a market launch. And swapping agencies without transferring account history, tracking documentation, and test learnings destroys institutional knowledge you paid to build.

If you want a second set of eyes on which of these is really biting, that's most of what my approach is: find the real constraint, define what good performance means, then decide whether the agency should be directed, coached, or replaced.

If the gap is ownership across channels, compare a fractional Head of Growth, an agency, and an in-house hire before choosing a replacement.

FAQ

How long should I give a struggling agency before replacing it?

Long enough for the changes to show up in the metrics that matter, which depends on your purchase cycle and how much of the funnel the agency controls. For a fast-moving DTC brand with reliable tracking, a written reset with 60 to 90 days of milestones is usually enough. If your model runs on repeat purchase and delayed payback, judge execution indicators and diagnosis quality first, because revenue is a lagging signal.

Isn't a drop in ROAS proof the agency is failing?

Usually not on its own. ROAS falls for reasons the agency doesn't touch: margin compression, discounting, stock-outs, checkout friction, softening demand, or a tracking change that reshaped attribution. Before you read a ROAS drop as an agency failure, confirm the numbers reconcile with your backend and that the agency controlled the lever that moved.

Should I hire a fractional head of growth instead of switching agencies?

If the recurring problem is that nobody sets the brief, targets, and measurement or holds the agency accountable, then yes, that gap is usually the constraint, not the agency itself. A fractional head of growth directs your existing agencies and specialists rather than replacing them, sets the commercial boundaries, and runs the review rhythm. Swapping agencies without fixing that leadership gap tends to reproduce the same result.

What if the new agency inherits the same problems?

That's exactly the risk, and it's why the reset comes before the replacement. A new agency handed an unclear brief, unrealistic targets, and untrustworthy data will optimize toward the same wrong number the last one did. Fix the foundations first so any agency, incumbent or new, is being measured against something real.
