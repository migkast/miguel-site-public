A channel-selection guide for $2M–$20M ecommerce brands, built on unit economics, demand conditions, and measurement you can trust.

Updated on: 2026-10-08

The right performance marketing mix for a $2M–$20M ecommerce brand is the smallest set of channels where the next dollar of spend produces profitable new-customer revenue within your payback window. That is the whole decision. It is not a fixed percentage split, not "Meta plus Google plus email," and not whatever your current agency is best at running. The mix follows from your margins, your demand, and your ability to produce the inputs each channel needs.

I keep running into the same situation. A brand doing $6M wants to "diversify channels," so they turn on TikTok, Pinterest, and a marketplace at once. Three months later, blended efficiency is worse, nobody can say which channel helped, and the real problem was a creative supply issue on Meta that nothing new was going to fix. Adding channels felt like progress. It was motion.

Start with the number that caps everything

Before you compare any channels, you need one figure: how much you can afford to pay to acquire a new customer. That comes from contribution margin, not revenue.

Take your net sales on a first order and subtract the variable costs: product cost, fulfillment, shipping subsidies, payment fees, returns, and the discounts you actually give. What is left is the money available to cover acquisition and still leave profit. Shopify's ecommerce CAC guidance puts contribution margin and payback at the center of acquisition math for exactly this reason.

A worked example, kept simple. If you hold 40% contribution margin before advertising, then $100 in net sales gives you $40 to work with. Spending $40 to generate that sale is roughly first-order break-even before overhead and profit. In revenue ROAS terms, that is 2.5x at the break-even line. This is an illustration, not a target. Plug in your own returns, discounts, and the payback period your cash position can tolerate.

Two things change the answer:

  • New-customer economics, not order efficiency. Cost per order blends in repeat buyers who were coming back anyway. You want cost per new customer, read against contribution on the first order and cohort payback. A channel can show a flattering revenue ROAS while mostly harvesting demand you already owned.
  • Revenue scale is context, not a budget. A $2M brand with 30% margins, tight cash, and slow repeat rates runs a different mix than a $20M brand with 55% margins and strong retention. The revenue band alone tells you almost nothing about what to spend.

If you cannot state your allowable CAC and payback window in one sentence, you are not ready to pick channels. You can read more on setting those numbers in this piece on profitable ecommerce targets.

Match the channel to your demand and your inputs

Every channel needs something to be true before it works. The failure I see most is turning on a channel whose precondition the brand cannot meet.

  • Search channels work when people already look for your product or category. If nobody searches for what you sell, search captures almost nothing.
  • Discovery-led social (Meta, TikTok) works when you can produce and refresh persuasive creative on a steady schedule. A good feed does not replace a good video.
  • Marketplace retail media works only if you sell on that marketplace with decent listings and economics.
  • Lifecycle marketing works when you have customers or opted-in prospects to talk to. No list, no leverage.

Hold each candidate against those conditions before you argue about budget.

Why "diversify" often makes things worse

Diversification sounds like risk management. In practice, at $2M–$20M, spreading spend across six channels usually means none of them get the creative volume, budget, or attention to work. You end up with six mediocre channels instead of two good ones.

The time to add a channel is when your core is constrained, not bored. If Meta efficiency drops only when you push past a certain spend level, that is a signal to test a new acquisition source. If Meta is fine and you are adding TikTok because a competitor is on it, that is a vanity decision.

Diagnose the constraint first. A weak result can come from creative supply, product economics, conversion rate, measurement gaps, inventory, or unclear ownership between your team and your agency. None of those get fixed by a new channel. If growth has stalled, work through where the real bottleneck sits before you touch the media plan.

Three numbers, three different questions

The measurement mistake that quietly wastes the most money is treating platform-reported ROAS as truth. Keep three views separate, because they answer different questions.

  1. Platform-reported channel results. Meta's ROAS, Google's conversion value. Useful for in-platform optimization, biased toward over-crediting each platform.
  2. Blended business results. Total net revenue against total marketing spend. This is the number that connects to your P&L and does not care which platform claims the sale.
  3. Incremental impact. What a channel actually added versus what would have happened anyway. This is the only view that justifies scaling.

Platform attribution and causal lift are not the same measurement. Meta itself distinguishes attribution-window reporting from its randomized Conversion Lift tests and warns the two are not directly comparable. Google describes incrementality testing as a controlled comparison between exposed and unexposed groups. Use these tests when the spend and the expected effect justify the effort. Do not line a test result up against platform ROAS as if they measure the same thing.

Media mix modeling is the other tool people reach for. Google's open-source Meridian framework models channel effects and response curves, but it needs rich history to be reliable. Google's own rule of thumb is roughly two years of weekly data for geo-level models and three for national. For a brand with sparse history and few channels, a full MMM is premature. Simple blended tracking plus occasional lift tests will serve you better until the data earns the model.

Allocate against the next dollar, not the average

The question is never "which channel has the highest average ROAS." It is "where does the next dollar do the most good." A channel can carry a beautiful blended average and still be saturated, meaning additional spend returns very little. Another can look average on paper and have real headroom.

So allocation becomes a set of decision rules you write down before you move budget:

  • The CAC or cost-per-new-customer ceiling each channel must stay under
  • The contribution and payback threshold that defines "working"
  • The test period and spend level for anything new
  • What evidence keeps, cuts, or expands the spend

Set those once and your weekly reviews stop being argument theatre. The channel either held the line or it did not. If this is new to how your team runs, the structure in this weekly growth meeting format makes the discipline stick.

What I would do first

If you are staring at this decision right now, in order:

  1. Write your allowable CAC and payback window in one sentence, using real variable costs.
  2. List your current channels and mark each as working, saturated, or unclear against that number.
  3. Fix the biggest constraint in your core before adding anything. Usually creative supply or conversion.
  4. Pick one new channel to test, only if the core is genuinely capped, and give it a fair budget and a fixed test window.
  5. Run at least one lift test on your largest channel this quarter, so you know how much of its reported ROAS is real.

That sequence beats any preset split, because it is anchored to your economics instead of someone else's benchmark.

A note on getting this right

Brands in the $2M–$20M range rarely fail because they picked the wrong channel. They fail because nobody set the commercial boundaries first, nobody owned the decision rules, and agencies optimized in isolation toward their own platform's numbers. The mix is downstream of leadership.

That is the gap I work in. Rather than replacing capable teams or agencies, the job is to set the commercial targets, give each channel a defined role and scaling rule, and hold the whole plan to incremental results instead of dashboard theatre. Across roughly 130 brands and work up to $250M in annual revenue, the pattern holds: the brands that scale profitably are the ones with clarity on the number and discipline on the rules, not the ones with the most channels switched on.

FAQ

What is the ideal marketing budget percentage for a $2M–$20M ecommerce brand?

There is no defensible universal percentage, and chasing one usually hurts you. A $2M brand with thin margins and slow repeat rates can afford far less than a $20M brand with strong retention. Set spend from allowable CAC, contribution, and payback, then let the total fall out of how much profitable demand each channel can absorb. A flat percentage ignores the costs that actually decide whether spend is profitable.

Should I run Meta and Google, or pick one?

For most brands in this band, both earn a place, because they do different jobs. Google captures existing intent; Meta creates and converts discovery demand. The real question is whether each is profitable on new-customer economics and whether either is saturated. Running both badly is worse than running one well, so staff creative and feed quality before you split budget.

How do I know if a channel's reported ROAS is real?

Run an incrementality test. Platform ROAS over-credits the platform by design, especially on retargeting and branded search. A geo or Conversion Lift test compares exposed and unexposed groups and shows what the channel actually added. Until you test, treat platform ROAS as a directional optimization signal, not proof of contribution.

When is it worth adding a fourth or fifth channel?

When your core channels are genuinely capped, meaning efficiency drops as you push more budget in and you have exhausted creative and conversion improvements. Adding a channel out of boredom, or because a competitor uses it, almost always dilutes focus. Diagnose the constraint first; a new channel rarely fixes a problem that lives in creative, economics, or conversion.
