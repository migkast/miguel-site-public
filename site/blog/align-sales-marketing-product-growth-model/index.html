A practical guide to building one shared, testable growth model across B2B SaaS teams, so everyone works from the same cause and effect.

Updated on: 2026-10-02

The fastest way to spot a misaligned growth org is to ask three leaders the same question: how many qualified opportunities did we create last month? In a misaligned company, you get three different numbers and three different definitions of "qualified." Sales counts deals they accepted. Marketing counts leads they passed. Product does not count at all, because product thinks this is a sales-and-marketing problem.

That gap is the real work. Aligning sales, marketing, and product around one growth model means agreeing on a single, testable explanation of how you create customer value and turn it into efficient, durable revenue. Not one dashboard. Not one KPI that replaces all others. A shared map of cause and effect that every function can reproduce, argue with, and act on.

What "one growth model" actually means

A growth model here is a quantitative picture of how acquisition, product value and activation, retention, monetization, and cost combine to produce growth. It is a management model, not a claim that every company has the same funnel.

The common mistake is reading "one model" as "one metric everyone optimizes." That breaks quickly. Teams can and should keep functional measures. Marketing watches pipeline creation. Sales watches win rate and cycle time. Product watches activation and adoption. The shared model is what connects those measures to a common outcome, and it stops any team from winning on their number while quietly wrecking someone else's.

So you end up with two artifacts:

  • The model: the causal map of inputs, loops, and outcomes.
  • The operating agreement: how the teams act on it, who owns what, and how disputes get settled.

Forrester's current Connected GTM thinking lands in a similar place, linking strategy, planning, execution requirements, execution, and measurement around shared customer and business outcomes rather than around department goals. The useful detail there: cross-functional alignment depends on feedback loops, not a one-time offsite where everyone nods.

Prerequisites most teams skip

Before you draw a single box, resolve these. Skipping them is why alignment projects become documentation nobody uses.

An accountable sponsor and a cross-functional owner. The sponsor resolves priority conflicts when sales and product disagree about what gets built or sold. A RevOps, growth, or equivalent lead coordinates the model and the implementation. One person owns decisions. Someone else can own the data.

A specific business problem and scope. Do not try to redesign the entire company. Pick a segment or a bottleneck. Self-serve-to-sales conversion for mid-market. New-logo acquisition in one vertical. A narrow scope makes data problems and bad incentives visible before they spread.

A usable baseline. Write down current definitions, data sources, performance, and known gaps. If your customer, opportunity, billing, and product data cannot be reconciled, that reconciliation is a workstream on its own. Until it is done, every dashboard disagreement looks like a team performance problem when it is really a data problem.

Agreement to use customer and financial evidence together. Sales notes, product usage, win/loss interviews, renewals, and unit economics each answer a different question. None of them describes the whole system alone.

Capacity to change workflows. A metric agreement that does not alter routing, planning, product decisions, or review meetings is not an implementation. It is a slide.

The nine steps, in order

The sequence matters. Tooling comes late, not first. Forrester puts data, technology, and workflow alignment as execution requirements that sit between planning and execution, not at the start. Buy tools before you settle definitions and you just automate the disagreement.

Step Work to do Output
1. Set the charter Name the sponsor, implementation owner, participating leaders, scope, decision rights, and review cadence. A short charter with named owners and the decisions the group can make.
2. Diagnose the system Map how target accounts discover, evaluate, buy, onboard, use, renew, and expand. Reconcile funnels and reports. Inspect lost deals, stalled opportunities, onboarding friction, churn. Baseline model, data-quality notes, ranked candidate constraints.
3. Agree on the customer Pick a priority ICP and the buying-group roles. Use interviews, sales calls, win/loss, usage, support themes, renewals. Agreed segment, customer problem hypothesis, buyer roles, evidence gaps.
4. Draw the causal model Work backward from the outcome to the inputs teams can influence. Make acquisition, activation, retention, monetization, expansion, and costs visible. One-page causal model with metric names, formulas, segments, owners.
5. Lock the metric contract Define entry/exit evidence per stage, record ownership, matching rules, required fields, and when stages can be revised. A metric dictionary and lifecycle rules approved by all three functions.
6. Build workflows Rules for routing, follow-up, sales feedback, usage alerts, onboarding escalation, and how customer evidence reaches product. Workflow and handoff rules with owners, triggers, and service expectations.
7. Instrument the minimum Connect only the systems and fields the model needs. Test capture before automating. Working instrumentation, a dashboard, and an exception report for bad data.
8. Pilot and revise Run the model on one segment. Review customer outcomes and economics next to process adoption. Pilot readout: what moved, what did not, data limits, next decision.
9. Set the operating rhythm A regular operating review for performance and decisions; a separate, less frequent strategy review for segment and investment choices. A recurring cross-functional review with a decision log and follow-through.

The temptation is to jump to step 7, because buying a tool feels like progress. Resist it. I have watched teams integrate a shiny attribution platform on top of four definitions of "lead" and end up more confused, with a more expensive way to be confused.

What belongs in the model and the scorecard

A workable model has one shared business outcome, a small set of leading inputs, and guardrails. That is it. Resist the instinct to add every metric someone likes.

Business outcome. New ARR, total ARR growth, or growth in a defined segment. Be explicit about whether you are chasing new logos, expansion, or both. Those pull in different directions.

Customer-value leading indicator. Pick a behavior that shows customers getting real value and that predicts future retention or revenue in your data. Amplitude's framing of a North Star metric as a customer-value measure that leads revenue is useful here. Their self-serve example is activated trial accounts with multiple active users, not trial sign-ups. Sign-ups are vanity. Activated accounts with real usage are a signal.

Acquisition and conversion inputs. Qualified account engagement, opportunity creation, stage conversion, cycle progression, win rate. Define each for the chosen segment and motion, not in the abstract.

Product and retention inputs. Time to first value, activation, recurring use of a core workflow, adoption across an account, renewal, expansion signals. Which ones apply depends on your product.

Economic guardrails. Acquisition cost and payback, gross or contribution margin, and net revenue retention. Stripe's working definitions are a fine starting point: CAC as sales and marketing cost over new customers acquired, and NRR as recurring revenue retained from a starting customer base including the net effect of expansion, contraction, and churn. Write down your own cost scope and cohort period rather than borrowing someone else's.

A clear equation. Something like: ending recurring revenue = starting recurring revenue + new-customer revenue + expansion − contraction − churn. Keep the assumptions visible. The equation is a management tool, not a universal law.

One warning on the North Star: do not pick it by taste. Test whether the candidate behavior actually tracks with later retention and revenue in your own cohorts. If it stops pointing toward revenue, change it. A pretty metric that does not predict money is a distraction with a nice name.

Where B2B alignment usually breaks

A few failure modes show up again and again, and all three functions contribute to them.

Treating the whole thing as a lead handoff. If the project is really just marketing-to-sales, product and retention stay outside the model. Then you have "aligned" sales and marketing feeding an acquisition machine that churns customers out the back. The topic includes product for a reason.

Using MQL and SQL as if they mean something universal. They do not. The same record can be qualified to one team and junk to another. Define observable entry criteria, ownership, and the next action locally. A PQL is worth defining only when product use genuinely signals buying intent. It is not a mandatory stage for every sales-led business.

Optimizing activity in isolation. Lead volume, clicks, features shipped, and bookings can all climb while customer value and margins flatten. Tie every team metric back to the shared outcome and the guardrails, or you will reward motion over progress.

Treating B2B buying as a linear funnel. Gartner describes six buying jobs - problem identification, solution exploration, requirements building, supplier selection, validation, and consensus creation - and buyers loop back through them. A rigid stage model reads normal buyer behavior as regression. Your model should tolerate that looping instead of punishing a rep for a deal that "went backward" when the buyer was just building internal consensus.

Treating attribution as truth. Tracking reports recorded touches. It cannot, on its own, prove every influence in a multi-person, nonlinear purchase. Use attribution directionally and triangulate with buyer-reported sources and sales evidence. Anyone presenting a single multi-touch model as the complete account of what drove revenue is selling certainty they do not have.

Sending sales requests straight to the roadmap. The loudest account is not the roadmap. Translate requests into underlying problems, then weigh reach, segment impact, strategic fit, and feasibility. Productboard's prioritization guidance makes the same point: frequency and affected segments matter more than raw request count.

How to verify it actually works

Set a baseline before you change anything. Then check both the business result and whether the operating system is functioning. Those are different things, and passing one without the other is misleading.

  • Metric agreement: all three teams can reproduce the same shared metric from the agreed definition and period. When numbers differ, you trace it to a source, timing, or logic issue, not to a dashboard argument.
  • Lifecycle integrity: records change stage only when the agreed evidence exists. Ownership, routing, and rejection reasons are observable.
  • Customer signal: the chosen value indicator improves for the target cohort and predicts later retention or revenue. More sign-ups is not proof.
  • Economics: conversion, cycle progression, new revenue, retention, CAC, and payback move in the right direction for the chosen segment.
  • Process reliability: handoffs complete, product feedback reaches the right owner, customer issues close the loop, and decisions from reviews get done.
  • Causal confidence: compare the pilot against its baseline and, where feasible, a matched cohort. Correlation is not the same as incremental impact.

A good result is not a prettier dashboard. It is a more dependable decision process connected to customer and business outcomes.

What I would do first

If I were starting Monday, I would not build the model. I would spend the first week reconciling definitions and data for one segment, and I would get the sponsor to sign a one-page charter naming who settles disputes. Nearly every alignment failure I have seen traces back to a missing owner for the hard call, or to data nobody trusted. Fix those two things and the model gets much easier to draw.

Then I would pick the narrowest meaningful scope, run the nine steps on that, and refuse to expand until the definitions and workflows hold up in practice.

This is the same discipline I bring to aligning finance, marketing, and growth in e-commerce work and to building a weekly growth review that actually produces decisions. The category differs. The principle does not: find the real constraint, make ownership clear, agree on the numbers, and build an operating rhythm that forces action. If you have strong teams and strong agencies but no shared model holding them together, the problem is rarely talent. It is the missing map and the missing referee.

FAQ

Do sales, marketing, and product need identical metrics to be aligned?

No, and forcing it usually backfires. Each team keeps functional measures that fit their work. What has to be shared is the outcome those measures ladder up to, plus guardrails that stop one team from winning at another's expense. Identical metrics would blur accountability. Connected metrics preserve it.

Who should own the growth model?

A cross-functional lead, often in RevOps or growth, coordinates the model and keeps the definitions honest. But ownership of the operations work is not the same as authority over strategic trade-offs. When sales and product disagree on what to prioritize, an executive sponsor settles it. An operations team cannot and should not arbitrate strategy.

Where does product fit if growth feels like a sales and marketing problem?

Product sits inside the model through activation, time to first value, adoption, retention, and expansion. If you leave product out, you optimize acquisition while customers leak out the back. A growth model that ignores retention and value realization is measuring half the system.

How long before a shared model shows results?

A bounded pilot on one segment usually shows whether the definitions and workflows hold within a quarter. Business outcomes like retention and payback take longer because they depend on cohorts maturing. Be suspicious of fast wins that come from redefining a metric rather than improving the underlying customer behavior.

Is attribution software required for this?

No. You need the capability to observe the agreed journey, and that capability is often spread across your existing CRM, product analytics, and billing systems. Buy new tooling only after the process and definitions are settled. Attribution helps directionally, but it does not replace customer-reported and sales evidence.
