How to Brief Your Ecommerce Agency to Hit Targets

Learn how to brief your ecommerce agency for profit-driven results. Set clear targets, roles, and measurement for campaigns that grow your business.

A practical guide to writing agency briefs that tie campaign work to profit, payback, and the numbers your business actually runs on.

Updated on: 2026-09-24

Most agency briefs I read are activity lists dressed up as strategy. "Scale Meta spend, refresh creative, improve email flows, test TikTok." Every line is a thing to do. Not one line tells the agency what winning looks like in money the business can spend. Then everyone is surprised when the quarter ends with more spend, more work, and roughly the same profit.

A good brief closes that gap. It translates your commercial reality into constraints and targets the agency can actually plan against. If you want your agency to hit your targets, you have to give them targets that map to your P&L, not to a dashboard.

Here is how I brief agencies when I want them to perform, and what I look for when a brief has gone wrong.

What a good agency brief actually contains

A brief that lets an agency hit commercial targets has four things most briefs skip: a profit target expressed in money, a payback window, a clear channel role, and the numbers you both agree to judge by.

Everything else is detail. If those four are missing, the agency will optimize toward whatever metric is easiest to move, usually ROAS or in-platform CPA, and you will get campaigns that look healthy on Meta and quietly lose money in your bank account.

Here is the difference in practice:

Weak brief line Strong brief line
"Improve ROAS on Meta" "Hold blended contribution margin above 22% at €140k/month spend"
"Scale spend aggressively" "Grow spend to €180k/month only while CAC payback stays under 90 days"
"Test new channels" "Prove TikTok can acquire first purchasers under €38 CAC before we scale past €15k/month"
"Grow revenue 30%" "Add €90k/month new-customer revenue at or above 2.1 blended MER"

The strong lines are harder to write because they force you to know your own economics first. That is the point. An agency cannot hit a target you have not defined.

Start with the money, not the channels

The most common briefing mistake is leading with channels. You start talking about Meta and Google before you have decided what the business needs those channels to produce.

Before you write a single channel instruction, you need three numbers agreed internally:

  • Contribution margin after variable costs. Not gross margin. What is left after COGS, shipping, payment fees, and fulfillment on a typical order. This is the money that pays for acquisition and everything else.
  • Acceptable payback window. How many days or months you are willing to wait to recover acquisition cost. A brand with strong repeat purchase can wait longer. A brand selling a one-time product cannot.
  • The commercial target itself. New-customer revenue, total revenue, or profit contribution for the period, with a spend ceiling attached.

If your team cannot produce those three cleanly, the problem is upstream of the agency. I have watched brands blame agencies for six months when the real issue was that nobody had agreed what "profitable" meant. If you are in that spot, fix the target-setting first. I have written more on setting profitable ecommerce targets that survive contact with a scaling budget.

Once these three numbers exist, the channel brief writes itself. The agency knows the ceiling, the margin they have to work inside, and how patient the business can be. That is a real planning constraint, not a vague ambition.

Give each channel a role, not just a budget

Handing an agency a lump budget and telling them to "make it work" produces predictable behavior. They pour money into whatever is converting cheapest today, which is almost always retargeting and branded search. Both look brilliant in the platform. Neither grows the business.

A brief should assign each channel a job:

  • Meta prospecting: acquire new customers at or below target CAC. Judged on new-customer volume and blended margin, not in-platform ROAS.
  • Google branded search: capture existing demand cheaply. Kept small and efficient, never counted as "growth."
  • Google non-brand and Shopping: capture in-market demand and defend category terms.
  • TikTok, Pinterest, Reddit, Snapchat: discovery and incremental reach, briefed as tests with a spend cap and a clear pass/fail bar before scaling.
  • Email and Klaviyo: retention, repeat purchase, and margin recovery. Judged on repeat rate and revenue per subscriber, not open rates.

When each channel has a role, you can tell whether the agency is doing its job on each one. When they all share a blended goal, everyone hides behind the average. A brand whose branded search "carries" a 4x blended ROAS is usually one honest cohort report away from a bad quarter.

The specific mix will differ by brand. The discipline of assigning roles does not.

Define what "good" means before the work starts

Half the agency conflicts I see come from an undefined bar. The agency thinks a 2.3 blended MER at €120k spend is a strong month. You think it is a disappointment. Neither of you wrote it down, so both of you are right, and the review meeting becomes an argument about vibes.

Write the bar into the brief. For each channel and for the account overall, state:

  • The target metric and the number that counts as on-track.
  • The floor that triggers a conversation.
  • The number that would justify scaling further.

This turns your reviews from opinion into checking against an agreed standard. It also protects a good agency from being blamed for hitting exactly what you asked for. If you want a template for the rhythm around this, the format I use for the weekly ecommerce growth meeting keeps these numbers in front of everyone so nobody relitigates the standard every week.

Fix your measurement before you blame the agency

You cannot brief to a target you cannot measure. If your attribution is a mess, the agency will optimize toward whatever the platform reports, and the platform always reports its own channel as the hero. Meta and Google both claim the same sale. Your reported ROAS across channels adds up to more revenue than you actually made.

Before you hold an agency to a CAC or margin number, make sure the measurement underneath it is solid enough to trust:

  • Blended numbers as the source of truth. Total spend against total new-customer revenue. It is boring and it does not lie the way platform-reported ROAS does.
  • Server-side tracking in place so signal loss is not quietly wrecking optimization and reporting.
  • A first-party view of CAC and payback by cohort, not just last-click attribution from ad platforms.
  • One agreed reporting source that both you and the agency look at, ideally not the ad platform's own dashboard.

This is the part most brands underinvest in, and it is where a lot of "agency underperformance" turns out to be measurement failure. Profitable-looking accounts with weak margins are almost always a measurement problem first. When the numbers are clean, the argument about whether the agency is performing gets short, because the answer is visible.

Write the operating rhythm into the brief

A brief is not a document you hand over once. The best briefs include how you will work together after signing: cadence, decision rights, and escalation.

Spell out:

  • Review cadence. Weekly for active accounts. What gets reviewed, who attends, what decisions get made in that meeting.
  • Decision rights. What the agency can change without asking. What needs sign-off. Budget shifts, new channel spend, creative direction, promotional depth.
  • Escalation rules. When a metric breaches its floor, what happens, and how fast.

Without this, you get one of two failure modes. Either the agency waits for permission on everything and moves at the speed of your inbox, or they change things freely and you find out in the monthly report that spend doubled on a channel you never approved. Both kill trust. Deciding the rules up front prevents both.

What I would do first

If your current agency relationship is producing activity without profit, do not start by rewriting the whole brief or shopping for a replacement. Do these three things in order:

  1. Agree your three numbers internally. Contribution margin, payback window, commercial target with a spend ceiling. This is your homework, not the agency's.
  2. Clean up the one measurement gap that matters most. Usually that is moving from platform-reported ROAS to blended CAC and payback. You do not need perfect attribution. You need numbers you both trust.
  3. Rewrite the brief around roles and floors. Give each channel a job and a pass/fail bar. Add the review cadence and decision rights.

Nine times out of ten, this turns a "we need to fire the agency" conversation into a "the agency now knows what we actually want" outcome. Replacing an agency is expensive and slow, and it does nothing if the brief that broke the last one is the same brief you hand the next one. If you have done all of this and the agency still cannot deliver, then the decision to replace the agency becomes clear and evidence-based rather than emotional.

This is most of what I do inside a brand as a fractional head of growth: sit above execution, translate the P&L into constraints the agency can plan against, and hold the standard in the weekly review. The agency keeps doing the work they are good at. The brief and the rhythm are what change.

FAQ

How detailed should an ecommerce agency brief be?

Detailed on outcomes, light on tactics. Be specific about commercial targets, margin floors, payback windows, channel roles, and the numbers you will judge by. Leave the how to the agency. If you are writing ad copy angles into the brief, you have gone too far into their job and not far enough into yours.

What should I do if my agency only reports platform ROAS?

Ask them to report against blended CAC, contribution margin, and payback instead. A good agency will welcome this because it protects them from being judged on a number that overstates their impact. If they resist showing blended numbers, that resistance tells you something worth knowing.

Can a better brief fix an agency that is genuinely underperforming?

Not always, but you often cannot tell which problem you have until the brief is clean. A vague brief hides both good and bad performance. Once you have defined targets, roles, and a trustworthy measurement source, underperformance becomes obvious fast. Fix the brief first so you are diagnosing the real problem, not the symptom.

How often should I review the brief with my agency?

Review results weekly against the agreed floors and targets. Revisit the brief itself quarterly, or whenever your commercial targets change. The weekly rhythm catches drift early. The quarterly review keeps the brief aligned with where the business is going rather than where it was.

Who should own the commercial targets, the agency or the brand?

The brand owns the targets. Contribution margin, payback tolerance, and profit goals are business decisions that depend on your cash position and appetite for risk. The agency owns the plan to hit them. When an agency sets its own targets, they will set ones they can comfortably clear, and that is not the same as ones that grow your business.
